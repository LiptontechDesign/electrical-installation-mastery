# QUESTION 1 — FULL MODEL ANSWER

## (a) Definitions — 6 marks

### i. Earth Electrode — 2 marks

Formal definition:

> **Earth electrode:** Conductive part, which may be embedded in the soil or in a specific conductive medium, e.g. concrete or coke, in electrical contact with the Earth.

### Practical meaning

An earth electrode creates an intentional conductive connection between an earthing system and the general mass of Earth.

Possible forms include:

- rod;
- plate;
- buried conductor;
- foundation-type electrode where applicable.

### Why it matters

In a TT-type arrangement, the electrode is a major part of the protective fault-current path.

A high electrode resistance can limit earth-fault current and raise touch voltage.

---

### ii. Residual Current Device — 2 marks

Formal definition:

> **Residual current device (RCD):** A mechanical switching device or association of devices intended to cause the opening of the contacts when the residual current attains a given value under specified conditions.

### Practical meaning

In a single-phase circuit, an RCD compares:

\[
I_L
\]

with:

\[
I_N
\]

In a healthy circuit:

\[
I_L\approx I_N
\]

If current leaks to Earth:

\[
I_L\neq I_N
\]

Residual current is:

\[
I_\Delta=|I_L-I_N|
\]

If the operating conditions are reached, the RCD disconnects.

### Important distinction

An RCD does not automatically provide overload or short-circuit protection unless it is part of a combined device such as an RCBO.

---

### iii. Ring Final Circuit — 2 marks

Formal definition:

> **Ring final circuit:** A final circuit arranged in the form of a ring and connected to a single point of supply.

### Practical meaning

The circuit leaves the origin, passes through the points/accessories and returns to the same point of supply.

This gives two conductor paths to points on the ring.

Because a ring can remain operational even if one conductor opens, ring continuity must be specifically tested.

---

## (b) Objectives of Earthing and Bonding — 3 marks

Any three:

1. provide a deliberate protective path for earth-fault current;
2. assist automatic operation of protective devices;
3. reduce dangerous touch voltages;
4. keep simultaneously accessible conductive parts at substantially the same potential;
5. reduce electric-shock risk.

### Strong explanation

During a line-to-metal fault, the protective path should allow sufficient fault current to flow so that the protective device disconnects.

At the same time, bonding helps reduce potential differences between accessible conductive parts.

---

## (c) Ohm's Law — 3 marks

For an ohmic conductor under substantially constant physical conditions:

\[
\boxed{V=IR}
\]

Rearrange:

\[
I=\frac{V}{R}
\]

### i. Voltage increases, resistance constant

\[
I\propto V
\]

Therefore current increases.

Example:

\[
V=12V,\quad R=4\Omega
\]

\[
I=\frac{12}{4}=3A
\]

Double voltage:

\[
V=24V
\]

\[
I=\frac{24}{4}=6A
\]

Voltage doubled and current doubled.

### ii. Resistance increases, voltage constant

\[
I\propto\frac1R
\]

Current decreases.

Example:

\[
12V,\quad2\Omega\Rightarrow6A
\]

\[
12V,\quad4\Omega\Rightarrow3A
\]

Resistance doubled and current halved.

## (e) Protective Devices — 4 marks

### i. MCB

An MCB is an overcurrent protective device.

It protects against:

- overload;
- short circuit;

when correctly selected and coordinated with the cable.

### ii. RCD

An RCD detects residual-current imbalance.

Its purpose includes fault/additional shock protection in the applications where it is required.

### iii. RCBO

An RCBO combines:

- residual-current protection;
- overload protection;
- short-circuit protection.

### iv. SPD

An SPD limits transient overvoltage and diverts surge current/energy.

It helps protect equipment from disturbances such as:

- lightning-related surges;
- switching transients.

# QUESTION 2 — FULL MODEL ANSWER

## (a) Suitable Domestic Arrangement — 15 marks

A distinction answer should show **design thinking**, not just list cable sizes from memory.

A sensible conceptual arrangement could divide the house into several final circuits.

### 1. Lighting circuits

Possible arrangement:

- ground-floor lighting circuit;
- first-floor lighting circuit.

Why separate them?

If one lighting circuit develops a fault, the whole house is not left in darkness.

The exterior light can be included on an appropriate lighting circuit or arranged separately depending on design and external conditions.

### 2. Socket circuits

Possible arrangement:

- ground-floor socket circuit;
- first-floor socket circuit.

The exact choice of radial or ring arrangement must be based on:

- expected load;
- cable route;
- installation method;
- voltage drop;
- applicable national requirements.

Do not write “all sockets are always 2.5 mm² on 32 A” as a universal rule.

### 3. Dedicated high-load circuits

Loads such as:

- water heater;
- cooker;
- other high-power fixed appliance

may require dedicated circuits.

Why?

Because a high continuous load can overload or unbalance a general-purpose socket circuit.

### 4. Protective devices

Each circuit requires appropriate protection against:

- overload;
- short circuit;
- earth fault.

Possible devices include:

- MCB;
- RCBO;
- RCD combined with suitable overcurrent protection.

Device rating must coordinate with the cable:

\[
I_B\leq I_N\leq I_Z
\]

where overload protection is required.

### 5. Protective earthing

A continuous CPC must reach all points that require protective earthing.

Class I metal equipment must have an effective CPC connection.

### 6. Additional residual-current protection

Where required by the applicable installation rules, suitable RCD/RCBO additional protection should be provided.

### 7. Outdoor equipment

The external entrance light must be:

- suitable for weather exposure;
- correctly IP-rated;
- mechanically suitable;
- correctly earthed if Class I;
- correctly protected.

## Two-Way Staircase Lighting — Detailed Explanation

A two-way system allows one lamp to be controlled from two positions.

A typical conceptual arrangement is:

```text
Permanent line
     |
    COM
   [SW1]
   /   \
 L1     L2
 |       |
 |       |    <- strappers
 |       |
 L1     L2
   [SW2]
    COM
     |
 Switched line
     |
 Luminaire
     |
 Neutral
```

### How it works

Each two-way switch is a changeover switch.

The COM terminal connects to either L1 or L2 depending on the switch position.

The two conductors between L1/L2 of the two switches are called strappers.

When the switch positions create a continuous path:

\[
\boxed{\text{lamp ON}}
\]

When they break the selected path:

\[
\boxed{\text{lamp OFF}}
\]

Operating either switch changes the selected path, so either switch reverses the lamp state.

### Important safety point

The switching should interrupt the line conductor.

Neutral goes to the load.

The CPC must remain continuous.

# QUESTION 3 — FULL MODEL ANSWER

## (a) Earthing — 2 marks

Formal definition:

> **Earthing:** Connection of the exposed-conductive-parts of an installation to the main earthing terminal of that installation.

### Practical meaning

Protective earthing provides a controlled path for fault current.

If a line conductor contacts exposed metalwork:

1. fault current flows through the protective path;
2. protective device operates;
3. dangerous touch voltage duration is reduced.

Earthing is therefore not simply “putting electricity into the ground.”

---

### i. Polarity — 2 marks

**Instrument/method:**

- low-resistance continuity tester for dead polarity checks;
- suitable approved voltage indicator where live supply-polarity verification is required.

**Purpose:**

Verify that:

- line reaches the correct terminals;
- neutral reaches neutral;
- single-pole switches interrupt line;
- socket outlets are correctly connected.

---

### ii. Insulation Resistance — 2 marks

**Instrument:**

\boxed{\text{insulation-resistance tester}}

**Purpose:**

Verify high resistance between conductors that should be electrically separated.

This helps detect:

- damaged insulation;
- moisture;

- incorrect connections.

**Instrument:**

## (c) Earth-Electrode Resistance Test Using Auxiliary Spikes — 6 marks

A strong answer should describe the **principle and sequence**.

### Step 1 — Make the test safe

Safely isolate where required and disconnect the electrode from parallel paths if the chosen test method requires an isolated electrode.

Why?

Parallel metallic paths can make the measured resistance falsely low.

### Step 2 — Connect the electrode under test

Connect the earth tester to the installation electrode.

### Step 3 — Place current auxiliary electrode

Drive the current spike into the soil at a suitable distance from the electrode under test.

### Step 4 — Place potential auxiliary electrode

Place a voltage/potential spike between the test electrode and the current spike.

### Step 5 — Carry out measurement

The tester injects a test current through the soil and measures the resulting voltage.

Using:

\[
R=\frac{V}{I}
\]

it determines the resistance.

### Step 6 — Verify the result

Move the potential spike somewhat closer to and farther from the electrode and repeat.

If the readings are substantially stable, the result is credible.

If readings vary significantly:

- spacing may be insufficient;
- soil resistance areas may overlap;
- reposition the electrodes and repeat.

### What the examiner wants

The important point is not just “put three rods in the ground.”

The candidate should understand:

\[
\boxed{\text{current electrode + potential electrode + stability check}}
\]

---

## (d) Reducing High Electrode Resistance — 2 marks

Any two:

- drive the electrode deeper;
- use additional properly spaced electrodes in parallel;
- choose a more suitable soil/location;
- increase effective electrode contact area;
- use an approved enhanced earthing system where permitted.

### Why spacing matters

Putting several rods extremely close together may not give the expected improvement because their resistance areas overlap.

# QUESTION 4 — FULL MODEL ANSWER

## (a) Electrical-Shock Casualty — 6 marks

A safe sequence is essential.

### 1. Do not touch the casualty while the source may be live

The casualty may form part of the circuit.

If you grab them, current may pass through you.

### 2. Make the electrical source safe

If it can be done safely:

- switch off;
- isolate;
- disconnect supply.

If the source cannot safely be isolated, keep away and obtain emergency/utility assistance.

### 3. Call for emergency help

Activate emergency medical services.

Ask another person to obtain an AED if available.

### 4. Assess responsiveness and breathing once safe

Check:

- responsiveness;
- normal breathing.

### 5. If not breathing normally / only gasping

Begin CPR according to current training.

Use the AED as soon as available.

### 6. Treat other injuries and monitor

Electrical casualties may also have:

- burns;
- falls;
- head injury;
- fractures;
- internal injury.

### Distinction principle

The first rule is:

\[
\boxed{\text{do not become the second casualty}}
\]

## (d) Protective Devices — 4 marks

### i. MCB

Protects against overcurrent:

- overload;
- short circuit.

### ii. RCD

Detects residual-current imbalance/leakage to Earth and disconnects under its specified operating conditions.

### iii. RCBO

Combines:

- RCD function;
- MCB-type overcurrent function.

## (b) Definitions — 6 marks

### i. Final Circuit — 2 marks

Formal definition:

> **Final circuit:** A circuit connected directly to current-using equipment, or to a socket-outlet or socket-outlets or other outlet points for the connection of such equipment.

### Practical meaning

It is the circuit that directly serves the final load.

A feeder from one distribution board to another is a distribution circuit, not a final circuit.

---

### ii. Ring Final Circuit — 2 marks

Formal definition:

> **Ring final circuit:** A final circuit arranged in the form of a ring and connected to a single point of supply.

### Practical meaning

The circuit leaves the origin and returns to the origin.

Each point normally has two current paths back toward the supply.

---

### iii. Spur — 2 marks

Formal definition:

> **Spur:** A branch from a ring or radial final circuit.

### Practical meaning

A spur leaves the main circuit path and does not itself continue as part of the main ring.

## Full Design Check

The selected design satisfies the supplied current-capacity and voltage-drop data.

But a real installation must still verify:

- fault protection;
- CPC;
- disconnection;
- earthing;
- protective-device breaking capacity;
- equipment suitability;
- applicable Kenya requirements.

This is a very important distinction-level point:

\[
\boxed{\text{passing one cable-sizing calculation does not complete the whole electrical design}}
\]

# C2 DISTINCTION ANSWER STANDARD

A distinction-level C2 candidate should consistently show:

## 1. Definitions

Give the exact technical definition first.

Then explain it in practical language.

## 2. Calculations

Never jump straight to the calculator answer.

Use:

\[
\boxed{
\text{GIVEN}
\rightarrow
\text{FORMULA}
\rightarrow
\text{SUBSTITUTE}
\rightarrow
\text{ANSWER}
\rightarrow
\text{CHECK}
}
\]

## 3. Earthing

Explain:

\[
\boxed{\text{fault path}\rightarrow\text{fault current}\rightarrow\text{protective-device operation}}
\]

Do not write only “earthing sends electricity to ground.”

## 4. Testing

For every test know:

- what instrument;
- dead or live;
- what it proves;
- what an abnormal result means.

## 5. Lighting

Understand the current path and switch logic rather than memorizing a picture.

## 6. Safe Isolation

A complete answer includes:

- identify;
- isolate;
- lock off;
- prove tester;
- prove dead;
- re-prove tester.

## 7. First Aid

The first action is not to grab the casualty.

Make the electrical scene safe first.

## 8. Cable Design

Understand the complete sequence:

\[
\boxed{
\text{load}
\rightarrow I_B
\rightarrow I_N
\rightarrow\text{correction factors}
\rightarrow I_t
\rightarrow\text{cable size}
\rightarrow\text{voltage drop}
}
\]
