# QUESTION 1 — FULL ANSWERS AND EXPLANATIONS

Each item carries **1 mark**, but the explanation below teaches the concept at the depth needed for oral follow-up questions.

## 1. Correct answer: **B**

A four-storey ordinary office building supplied at low-voltage three-phase falls within the present published C1 technical assessment scope, subject to any other applicable restriction.

The essential C1 scope points are:

- low-voltage;
- three-phase;
- buildings up to four storeys;
- excludes factories;
- excludes places designated for public entertainment.

### Why the other choices fail

- **A — six storeys:** exceeds the stated four-storey C1 scope.
- **C — factory:** factories are excluded from the stated C1 scope.
- **D — nightclub:** a nightclub is a place of public entertainment and falls within the stated exclusion.

### Exam habit

When licence-scope questions appear, do not answer from voltage alone. Check:

\[
\boxed{\text{phase system + building height + building use + exclusions}}
\]

---

## 2. Correct answer: **B — Energy meter**

An energy meter measures electrical energy used over time, commonly in:

\[
\boxed{kWh}
\]

A wattmeter measures instantaneous real power in watts.

The relationship is:

\[
E=Pt
\]

Therefore an energy meter effectively accumulates power use over time.

---

## 3. Correct answer: **C — Zero**

In a perfectly balanced three-phase star system:

- the three phase currents have equal magnitude;
- they are displaced by \(120^\circ\);
- their vector sum is zero.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{I_N=0}
\]

in the ideal balanced case.

### Important distinction

This does **not** mean neutral is always zero in a real installation. Neutral current can arise from:

- unequal single-phase loading;
- harmonics;
- faults;
- poor connections.

---

## 4. Correct answer: **B — \(V_L=V_{ph}\)**

For a balanced delta connection:

\[
\boxed{V_L=V_{ph}}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{I_L=\sqrt3\,I_{ph}}
\]

Why? Each winding is connected directly between two line conductors, so each winding receives the full line-to-line voltage.

---

## 5. Correct answer: **B — Reduces supply current**

For a balanced three-phase load:

\[
P=\sqrt3V_LI_L\cos\phi
\]

Rearrange:

\[
I_L=\frac{P}{\sqrt3V_L\cos\phi}
\]

If:

- real power \(P\) stays the same;
- voltage stays the same;
- power factor increases;

then the denominator increases and current falls.

This reduces:

- cable heating;
- voltage drop;
- kVA demand;
- transformer loading.

---

## 6. Correct answer: **C — 1000 rpm**

Use:

\[
N_s=\frac{120f}{P}
\]

Given:

\[
f=50Hz,\qquad P=6
\]

Substitute:

\[
N_s=\frac{120\times50}{6}
\]

\[
N_s=\frac{6000}{6}
\]

\[
\boxed{N_s=1000rpm}
\]

### Check

More poles produce lower synchronous speed. A six-pole motor must run slower than a four-pole 50 Hz motor at 1500 rpm, so 1000 rpm is reasonable.

---

## 7. Correct answer: **B — Interchange any two line conductors**

Changing any two phases reverses the phase sequence.

For example:

\[
L1-L2-L3
\]

becomes:

\[
L1-L3-L2
\]

The rotating magnetic field reverses, so a normal three-phase induction motor reverses rotation.

---

## 8. Correct answer: **C — Metallic enclosure**

The exposed metal enclosure of Class I equipment is connected to the protective conductor.

If insulation fails and line touches the enclosure:

1. enclosure becomes live;
2. fault current flows through the CPC;
3. protective device should disconnect;
4. duration of dangerous touch voltage is limited.

The winding itself is not “earthed” as the normal protective measure.

---

## 9. Correct answer: **B — Phase-sequence indicator**

A phase-sequence indicator identifies the order of the three phase voltages.

It is used when phase order matters, especially for:

- motors;
- pumps;
- fans;
- compressors;
- phase-sensitive equipment.

---

## 10. Correct answer: **B — Residual-current imbalance**

In a healthy single-phase circuit:

\[
I_L\approx I_N
\]

If some current leaks to Earth:

\[
I_L\neq I_N
\]

The RCD detects the residual difference:

\[
I_\Delta=|I_L-I_N|
\]

and disconnects when the operating conditions are met.

### Key distinction

An RCD is not automatically an overload-protection device.

---

## 11. Correct answer: **C — \(I_B\leq I_N\leq I_Z\)**

Where overload protection is required:

\[
\boxed{I_B\leq I_N\leq I_Z}
\]

where:

- \(I_B\) = circuit design current;
- \(I_N\) = rated current of protective device;
- \(I_Z\) = current-carrying capacity of the cable under installed conditions.

### Meaning

- If \(I_N<I_B\), the protective device may trip in normal service.
- If \(I_N>I_Z\), the cable may overload before the protective device disconnects.

---

## 12. Correct answer: **A — Sustained motor overload**

A thermal overload relay is intended to protect a motor from excessive thermal stress caused by sustained overcurrent.

Possible causes include:

- excessive mechanical load;
- locked/stalled condition;
- single phasing;
- prolonged starting.

It is not a substitute for a short-circuit protective device.

---

## 13. Correct answer: **B — Increase**

Approximate fault current is:

\[
I_f=\frac{U_0}{Z_s}
\]

Therefore if \(Z_s\) decreases:

\[
I_f\uparrow
\]

A larger earth-fault current generally causes an overcurrent device to operate more quickly.

---

## 14. Correct answer: **C — 1500 rpm**

\[
N_s=\frac{120\times50}{4}
\]

\[
N_s=\frac{6000}{4}
\]

\[
\boxed{1500rpm}
\]

A practical induction motor with four poles would normally run slightly below this speed because of slip.

---

## 15. Correct answer: **B — Delta**

A nameplate marked:

\[
400/690V\quad\Delta/Y
\]

means the winding is designed for about 400 V.

At 400 V line voltage:

\[
\boxed{\Delta}
\]

because:

\[
V_{ph}=V_L=400V
\]

At 690 V line voltage, star gives:

\[
V_{ph}=\frac{690}{\sqrt3}\approx398V
\]

---

## 16. Correct answer: **B — One-third**

During star starting, each winding receives:

\[
\frac{V_L}{\sqrt3}
\]

instead of full line voltage in delta.

Under the usual simplified comparison, star line starting current is about:

\[
\boxed{\frac13}
\]

of the delta/DOL starting line current.

---

## 17. Correct answer: **C — Induction motor**

An induction motor needs magnetizing current to create magnetic flux.

That current is inductive and lags voltage.

Therefore the motor normally has:

\[
\boxed{\text{lagging power factor}}
\]

---

## 18. Correct answer: **C — \(N_s=120f/P\)**

Remember:

\[
\boxed{N_s=\frac{120f}{P}}
\]

A good check is dimensional reasoning:

- frequency increases → speed increases;
- number of poles increases → speed decreases.

---

## 19. Correct answer: **B — approximately 230 V**

For star:

\[
V_{ph}=\frac{V_L}{\sqrt3}
\]

\[
V_{ph}=\frac{400}{1.732}
\]

\[
\boxed{V_{ph}\approx231V}
\]

---

## 20. Correct answer: **A — Real power required by the load**

Power-factor correction mainly reduces:

- reactive power drawn from the supply;
- apparent power;
- line current.

The real mechanical/electrical load remains approximately the same.

\[
\boxed{kW\approx\text{unchanged}}
\]

## (c) Preventing Inadvertent Energization — 4 marks

A distinction answer might say:

1. **Identify the correct section and every possible source.**  
   This includes main supply, generators, UPS systems, PV, backfeeds and interconnections.

2. **Isolate the section using a suitable isolating device.**

3. **Apply a personal lock-off device.**  
   The isolation should not be capable of being casually restored by another person.

4. **Attach a warning tag/notice.**

5. **Retain control of the lock-off key.**

6. **Prove dead using an approved voltage indicator.**

7. **Re-prove the tester after the dead test.**

8. **Communicate the isolation to affected personnel.**

### Why this matters

A breaker being OFF is not proof that the equipment is dead.

Possible dangers include:

- wrong breaker;
- faulty labelling;
- alternative supply;
- borrowed neutral;
- another person re-closing the device.

# QUESTION 3 — FULL MODEL ANSWER

## (a) Equipotential Bonding — 2 marks

Formal definition:

> **Equipotential bonding:** Electrical connection maintaining various exposed-conductive-parts and extraneous-conductive-parts at substantially the same potential.

### What this means physically

The purpose is not primarily to carry normal load current.

Bonding reduces the voltage difference a person could bridge between two simultaneously accessible conductive parts.

Example:

- metal water pipe;
- exposed metal enclosure of electrical equipment.

If both are maintained near the same potential during a fault, the dangerous touch-voltage difference is reduced.

---

## (b) Safety Purposes of Protective Equipotential Bonding — 2 marks

Any two:

1. reduce dangerous touch-voltage differences;
2. keep simultaneously accessible conductive parts at substantially the same potential;
3. support protection against electric shock;
4. support the effectiveness of the overall ADS/earthing arrangement.

### Distinction explanation

Earthing and bonding are related but not identical:

- **earthing** provides a path back toward the source/Earth reference;
- **bonding** reduces potential differences between conductive parts.

---

## (c) CPC — 2 marks

Formal definition:

> **Circuit protective conductor (cpc):** A protective conductor connecting exposed-conductive-parts of equipment to the main earthing terminal.

### Practical meaning

The CPC:

- normally carries no load current;
- provides the protective path during a line-to-metal fault;
- helps fault current reach a level that causes automatic disconnection.

A visible green/yellow conductor is not enough; continuity must be verified.

---

## (d) Earthing Systems — 4 marks

### i. TN-C-S

In a TN-C-S arrangement:

- the source neutral is earthed;
- neutral and protective functions are combined in a PEN conductor over part of the supply;
- they are separated into distinct neutral and protective conductors at/near the consumer installation.

During a fault, current returns principally through a metallic protective path.

### Safety significance

A broken/high-resistance PEN conductor can be dangerous because consumer protective metalwork may rise in potential.

---

### ii. TT

In a TT system:

- the source has its own earth connection;
- the consumer has a local earth electrode;
- exposed-conductive-parts are connected to that electrode.

The earth-fault path includes the soil/electrode system.

Because this path can have relatively high impedance, fault current may be too low to operate an ordinary overcurrent device quickly.

Therefore RCD fault protection is commonly very important.

---

## (e) Earth Electrode — 2 marks

Formal definition:

> **Earth electrode:** Conductive part, which may be embedded in the soil or in a specific conductive medium, e.g. concrete or coke, in electrical contact with the Earth.

### Practical meaning

The electrode forms an intentional electrical connection between the installation earthing system and the general mass of Earth.

---

## (f) Test Purposes — 4 marks

### i. Earth-electrode resistance test — 2 marks

Purpose:

- determine the resistance of the earth electrode/earthing arrangement to the general mass of Earth;
- assess whether the electrode is effective for the intended earthing/protection arrangement.

A very high or unstable result may indicate:

- poor electrode contact;
- dry/high-resistivity soil;
- damaged electrode;
- inadequate electrode depth/arrangement.

### ii. Earth-fault loop impedance test — 2 marks

Purpose:

- determine the total impedance of the fault-current loop;
- assess whether sufficient fault current can flow for the protective device to disconnect within the required condition/time.

The fundamental relationship is:

\[
I_f\approx\frac{U_0}{Z_s}
\]

Therefore:

\[
Z_s\uparrow\Rightarrow I_f\downarrow
\]

and protective operation may become slower.

---

## (g) Floating/Open Neutral — 4 marks

A floating/open neutral is a neutral conductor or neutral point that has lost its secure reference connection to the source neutral.

### Danger 1 — Voltage displacement

In an unbalanced three-phase four-wire system, the star point can shift.

Some single-phase loads can experience:

- overvoltage;
- undervoltage.

This can damage appliances.

### Danger 2 — Neutral may become dangerous

A disconnected neutral can rise toward line potential through connected loads.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{neutral must never automatically be assumed safe}}
\]

### Corrective action

- isolate safely;
- locate the open/high-resistance neutral;
- repair/restore the connection;
- test the installation before re-energizing.

# QUESTION 4 — FULL MODEL ANSWER

## (a) Causes of Poor Lagging Power Factor — 2 marks

Any two:

- induction motors;
- lightly loaded motors;
- transformers;
- discharge/inductive lighting;
- magnetic ballasts;
- other inductive loads.

### Why

Inductive equipment needs magnetizing current.

Current therefore lags voltage.

---

## (b) Disadvantages of Poor Power Factor — 2 marks

Any two:

- higher line current for same kW;
- higher \(I^2R\) cable losses;
- greater voltage drop;
- larger cable requirement;
- larger switchgear rating;
- greater transformer/generator kVA loading;
- reduced usable system capacity.

---

## (c) Power-Factor Improvement Methods — 2 marks

Any two:

- shunt capacitor bank;
- automatic capacitor bank;
- synchronous condenser;
- appropriately operated synchronous machine.

Most ordinary installations use capacitors because they supply leading reactive current that offsets part of the lagging reactive requirement.

## (e) Purpose of Insulation-Resistance Testing — 1 mark

To verify that conductors which should be electrically separated are separated by sufficiently high insulation resistance.

It helps reveal:

- damaged insulation;
- moisture;
- contamination;
- trapped cables;
- unintended conductive paths.

---

## (f) Precautions Before IR Testing — 3 marks

Any three:

1. safely isolate and prove dead;
2. disconnect sensitive electronic equipment/SPDs where necessary;
3. select the correct test voltage;
4. ensure no person is touching conductors;
5. discharge capacitive equipment before and after testing;
6. place switches/controls in positions that include the intended wiring;
7. disconnect loads that would distort the result.

### Why this matters

An insulation tester may apply hundreds of volts DC.

It can damage electronics or produce misleading results if equipment remains connected.

# QUESTION 5 — FULL MODEL ANSWER

## (a) Motor-Starting Methods — 2 marks

Any two:

- star-delta;
- autotransformer;
- soft starter;
- VFD;
- rotor-resistance starter for a wound-rotor motor.

---

## (b) DOL Limitations — 2 marks

Any two:

1. high starting current;
2. possible voltage dip in the supply;
3. high mechanical shock/torque;
4. unsuitable for some large motors or weak supplies.

### Explanation

At standstill an induction motor has high slip and draws a large current.

DOL applies full line voltage immediately.

---

## (c) Protective Devices — 3 marks

### i. Thermal overload relay

Protects against sustained motor overload and excessive thermal stress.

It is intended for abnormal running current, not severe short-circuit interruption.

### ii. Short-circuit protective device

Interrupts very high fault current caused by short circuits.

Examples:

- fuse;
- circuit-breaker.

It must have sufficient breaking capacity for the prospective fault current.

### iii. Phase-failure relay

Detects loss of a phase or serious phase abnormality and trips/prevents running.

This helps protect against single phasing, which can overheat the remaining windings.

---

## (d) Slip — 2 marks

Slip is the difference between synchronous speed and rotor speed, usually expressed as a percentage of synchronous speed.

\[
\boxed{
s=\frac{N_s-N_r}{N_s}\times100\%
}
\]

### Why slip is necessary

The stator rotating magnetic field must move relative to the rotor in order to induce rotor EMF and rotor current.

If:

\[
N_r=N_s
\]

then relative motion would be zero.

No induced rotor current would be produced, so electromagnetic torque would fall away.

Therefore a normal induction motor runs slightly below synchronous speed.

---

## (e) Speed and Slip — 3 marks

Given:

\[
f=50Hz
\]

\[
P=4
\]

\[
N_r=1440rpm
\]

### i. Synchronous speed

\[
N_s=\frac{120f}{P}
\]

\[
N_s=\frac{120\times50}{4}
\]

\[
\boxed{N_s=1500rpm}
\]

### ii. Percentage slip

\[
s=
\frac{1500-1440}{1500}
\times100
\]

\[
s=
\frac{60}{1500}
\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{s=4\%}
\]

### Check

A running induction motor speed lower than synchronous speed is expected.

## (f) 400/690 V Δ/Y Motor — 2 marks

On a 400 V supply:

\[
\boxed{\Delta}
\]

Reason:

In delta:

\[
V_{ph}=V_L
\]

so each winding receives:

\[
400V
\]

If permanently connected in star:

\[
V_{ph}=\frac{400}{\sqrt3}\approx231V
\]

which under-voltages a winding intended for approximately 400 V normal running.

---

## (g) VFD Speed Control — 2 marks

A VFD changes the supply frequency to the motor.

Because:

\[
N_s=\frac{120f}{P}
\]

changing \(f\) changes synchronous speed.

A good VFD also controls voltage appropriately so motor flux remains suitable.

This allows:

- controlled acceleration;
- deceleration;
- adjustable speed;
- reduced mechanical shock.

---

## (h) Star-Delta Current and Torque Reduction — 2 marks

During star starting:

\[
V_{phase}=\frac{V_L}{\sqrt3}
\]

So the winding voltage is only about 57.7% of delta voltage.

This reduces starting current.

Under the normal simplified comparison:

\[
I_{L,star}\approx\frac13I_{L,delta}
\]

Motor torque is approximately proportional to voltage squared:

\[
T\propto V^2
\]

Therefore:

\[
T_{star}\approx\frac13T_{delta}
\]

This is why star-delta is unsuitable for loads needing high starting torque.

---

## (i) Motor Hums but Does Not Accelerate — 2 marks

Any two likely causes:

- single phasing/loss of one phase;
- locked/jammed rotor;
- severe undervoltage;
- incorrect star/delta connection;
- faulty contactor contact;
- winding fault;
- excessive mechanical load.

### Correct troubleshooting approach

Do not keep repeatedly energizing the motor.

Instead:

1. isolate safely;
2. verify supply and phase continuity;
3. inspect mechanical load;
4. test windings/insulation as appropriate;
5. verify starter/control operation.
