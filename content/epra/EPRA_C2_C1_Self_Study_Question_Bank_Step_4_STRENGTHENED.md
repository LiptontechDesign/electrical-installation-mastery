# Section A — Initial Verification and Periodic Inspection

## Question 1 — Define Initial Verification
**What is meant by initial verification of an electrical installation? (4 marks)**

### Answer

### Formal BS 7671 definition

The uploaded Part 2 defines **Verification** as:

> **“All measures by means of which compliance of the electrical installation with the relevant requirements of BS 7671 are checked, comprising inspection, testing and certification.”**

Initial verification therefore brings together:

1. inspection;
2. testing;
3. assessment of results;
4. certification

a new electrical installation, or new work forming part of an installation, before it is put into service.

The purpose is to verify, so far as is reasonably practicable, that the installation complies with the applicable safety requirements.

### BS 7671 Reference

**Regulation 641.1**

Every installation is required to be inspected and tested during erection and on completion before being put into service.

### Related formal definition

The uploaded Part 2 defines **Inspection** as:

> **“Examination of an electrical installation using all the senses as appropriate.”**

That definition explains why inspection is not only “looking.” Depending on the task it can include seeing, touching where safe, smelling signs of overheating, and other appropriate sensory examination before instruments are relied upon.

---

## Question 2 — Correct General Order
**Put the following activities in the correct overall order:**

- certification;
- testing;
- inspection.

**(3 marks)**

### Answer

The correct sequence is:

\[
\boxed{\text{Inspection}\rightarrow\text{Testing}\rightarrow\text{Certification}}
\]

Inspection comes first because many defects can be identified before instruments are connected.

Testing then confirms electrical characteristics that cannot be established reliably by visual inspection alone.

Certification is completed after the relevant inspection and testing have demonstrated satisfactory results.

---

## Question 3 — Why Inspect Before Testing?
**Give five reasons why visual inspection should be carried out before electrical testing. (5 marks)**

### Answer

Visual inspection can identify defects such as:

1. damaged equipment;
2. exposed copper;
3. loose or missing covers;
4. incorrect conductor identification;
5. unsuitable protective devices;
6. missing CPCs;
7. poor terminations;
8. missing barriers;
9. incorrect cable type;
10. signs of overheating or burning.

Inspection first is important because a serious visible defect may make subsequent testing unsafe or meaningless.

For example, there is little value in performing detailed live testing on equipment that is visibly damaged and unsafe to energize.

---

## Question 4 — Define Periodic Inspection and Testing
**What is periodic inspection and testing, and how does it differ from initial verification? (5 marks)**

### Answer

Periodic inspection and testing is the inspection and testing of an **existing installation** at an appropriate time after it has been put into service.

Its purpose is to determine whether the installation is in a satisfactory condition for continued use.

Initial verification concerns:

\[
\boxed{\text{new work before first service}}
\]

Periodic inspection concerns:

\[
\boxed{\text{existing work already in service}}
\]

Periodic inspection also has to consider:

- deterioration;
- damage;
- alterations;
- environmental effects;
- signs of misuse;
- changes in loading;
- and defects that may have developed over time.

---

## Question 5 — Older Installation
An installation was correctly designed to an earlier edition of a wiring standard and is still functioning safely.

**Should it automatically be declared unsafe simply because a newer edition now exists? Explain. (5 marks)**

### Answer

No.

A newer edition of a standard does not automatically mean that every installation designed to an earlier edition has suddenly become unsafe.

The inspector should assess the **actual condition and safety of the installation**.

Important questions include:

- does it remain safe?
- has it deteriorated?
- are protective measures still effective?
- have later alterations created problems?
- does any non-compliance now give rise to danger?

Age or an earlier design standard alone is not proof of danger.

---

## Question 6 — Competence and Test Instruments
**State five requirements or good practices relating to the instruments used for electrical inspection and testing. (5 marks)**

### Answer

Suitable points include:

1. the correct instrument must be selected for the test;
2. the instrument must be suitable for the voltage and installation category;
3. test leads and probes must be in good condition;
4. the instrument must be functioning correctly;
5. calibration or verification of accuracy must be current where required;
6. the operator must know how to use it correctly;
7. batteries must be adequate;
8. instrument limitations must be understood.

A test result is only useful if the instrument and the person using it are both reliable.

# Section B — Safe Isolation

## Question 7 — Define Safe Isolation
**What is safe isolation? (4 marks)**

### Answer

### Formal BS 7671 definition of isolation

The uploaded Part 2 defines **Isolation** as:

> **“A function intended to cut off for reasons of safety the supply from all, or a discrete section, of the installation by separating the installation or section from every source of electrical energy.”**

Safe isolation is the practical controlled process that applies that function by:

- identifying the correct circuit/equipment;
- disconnecting it from every relevant source;
- securing the isolation against inadvertent reconnection;
- and proving that the conductors to be worked on are dead.

Its purpose is to prevent electric shock, burns, arc injury and accidental re-energization while work is in progress.

---

## Question 8 — Safe-Isolation Sequence
**Describe a complete safe-isolation procedure for a single-phase circuit. (12 marks)**

### Answer

A strong sequence is:

1. obtain permission where necessary to interrupt the supply;
2. identify the correct circuit and means of isolation;
3. inform affected persons where necessary;
4. check the condition of the voltage indicator and proving unit;
5. prove the voltage indicator works;
6. switch off the correct circuit using a suitable isolation device;
7. confirm that the expected load has stopped operating where this assists identification;
8. secure the isolating device in the OFF position with a suitable lock-off device;
9. attach an appropriate warning notice;
10. retain control of the lock-off key;
11. prove dead at the point of work by testing all relevant conductor combinations;
12. re-prove the voltage indicator on the proving unit or known live source;
13. begin work only after the circuit has been confirmed dead.

The essential principle is:

\[
\boxed{\text{Prove tester}\rightarrow\text{prove dead}\rightarrow\text{re-prove tester}}
\]

---

## Question 9 — Why Prove the Tester Before Use?
**Why must a voltage indicator be proved before it is used to prove a circuit dead? (4 marks)**

### Answer

A defective tester may falsely indicate zero voltage on a live circuit.

Possible faults include:

- flat batteries where applicable;
- damaged test leads;
- broken probes;
- internal instrument failure.

Testing the voltage indicator on a proving unit or suitable known live source confirms that it is capable of detecting voltage immediately before the dead test.

Without this check, a zero reading cannot safely be trusted.

---

## Question 10 — Why Re-Prove the Tester?
**Why must the voltage indicator be proved again after the dead test? (4 marks)**

### Answer

The tester may have failed during the test.

If it is not re-proved, a false zero reading could be caused by:

- tester failure;
- damaged lead;
- poor probe contact;
- instrument malfunction.

Re-proving it after the test confirms that the instrument was still capable of detecting voltage.

This completes the:

\[
\boxed{\text{prove-test-prove}}
\]

principle.

---

## Question 11 — Suitable Instrument for Proving Dead
**What type of instrument is preferred for proving the presence or absence of voltage on a low-voltage circuit? (4 marks)**

### Answer

A suitable proprietary:

\[
\boxed{\text{two-pole voltage detector}}
\]

or approved test lamp designed for the purpose is preferred.

The device and its probes should be appropriate for the system voltage and installation category.

For proving dead, a general-purpose multimeter is less desirable because:

- it can be left on the wrong range;
- it can be incorrectly connected;
- operator error is easier.

A non-contact “volt stick” should not be relied upon as the sole means of proving a circuit dead.

---

## Question 12 — Single-Phase Test Combinations
After isolation of a single-phase circuit, which conductor combinations should be checked to prove dead? **(3 marks)**

### Answer

The relevant combinations are:

1. line to neutral;
2. line to protective conductor/Earth;
3. neutral to protective conductor/Earth.

All should indicate approximately:

\[
\boxed{0V}
\]

before the circuit is regarded as safely isolated.

The neutral-to-earth test is essential because it can reveal a dangerous reverse-polarity condition that a line-to-neutral check alone might miss.

---

## Question 13 — Why Line-to-Neutral Alone Is Not Enough
A circuit is isolated at a single-pole MCB.

A test between line and neutral reads 0 V.

**Explain why the circuit must not yet be declared dead. (6 marks)**

### Answer

A 0 V line-to-neutral reading does not prove that neither conductor is live.

If line and neutral have been reversed upstream:

- the conductor believed to be neutral may actually be live;
- a single-pole MCB may disconnect the conductor that is actually at neutral potential;
- the unswitched conductor may remain energized.

Both conductors could therefore be at the same potential relative to each other, giving:

\[
L-N=0V
\]

while one of them remains dangerous relative to Earth.

This is why the complete checks include:

\[
L-N,\quad L-E,\quad N-E
\]

---

## Question 14 — Reverse Polarity and Single-Pole Isolation
**Explain why reverse polarity can make isolation by a single-pole device dangerous. (6 marks)**

### Answer

A single-pole protective device is intended to interrupt the line conductor.

If the supply conductors have been reversed:

- the protective device may actually interrupt the neutral;
- the conductor believed to be neutral may remain connected to the live supply;
- equipment may stop functioning even though parts of the circuit remain energized.

Therefore the fact that:

- a lamp goes out;
- a socket stops working;
- or an MCB is OFF

does not by itself prove safe isolation.

The circuit must always be **proved dead with an approved voltage indicator**.

---

## Question 15 — Lock-Off Key
**Why should the person carrying out the work retain control of the isolation key? (4 marks)**

### Answer

The person working on the circuit must remain in control of the isolation.

If another person has access to the key, the circuit could be re-energized while work is in progress.

Retaining the key helps ensure:

\[
\boxed{\text{the circuit cannot be restored without the worker's control}}
\]

A warning label alone is not equivalent to secure isolation because someone may ignore or remove it.

---

## Question 16 — Leaving the Work Area
A circuit has been isolated and proved dead. The electrician leaves the work area for some time and later returns.

**What should be done before touching conductors again? Explain. (4 marks)**

### Answer

The isolation should be checked again and the circuit should be re-proved dead.

The electrician cannot safely assume that conditions remained unchanged while absent.

Possible events include:

- someone interfering with the isolation;
- an additional source being connected;
- a lock-off arrangement being disturbed;
- incorrect circuit identification.

Safe working relies on verifying the present condition, not on assuming that an earlier test is still valid.

---

## Question 17 — Three-Phase Proving-Dead Sequence
A three-phase four-wire circuit has L1, L2, L3, N and PE.

**How many unique conductor-to-conductor combinations are there for a complete proving-dead check, and list them. (12 marks)**

### Answer

There are:

\[
\boxed{10}
\]

unique combinations.

A systematic sequence is:

### To Earth

1. L1-E
2. L2-E
3. L3-E
4. N-E

### To Neutral

5. L1-N
6. L2-N
7. L3-N

### Between Phases

8. L1-L2
9. L1-L3
10. L2-L3

A systematic sequence is safer than random testing because it reduces the chance of accidentally omitting a conductor pair.

During a periodic inspection, a 20 mm knockout is left open in an enclosure where finger protection is required.

Which is the better observation?

A. “Fit a blanking plug.”

Explain. **(4 marks)**

**Why should damaged electrical equipment be identified before energization? (4 marks)**

### Answer

Damage may compromise:

- insulation;
- enclosure integrity;
- protective earthing;
- mechanical safety;
- fire protection;
- safe operation.

Energizing damaged equipment may create:

- shock risk;
- short circuit;
- earth fault;
- arc fault;
- fire.

Testing does not replace the need for physical inspection.

# Step 4 Distinction Lens — What Testing Is Trying to Prove

The uploaded BS 7671 Part 2 defines:

> **Basic protection:** “Protection against electric shock under fault-free conditions.”

and:

> **Fault protection:** “Protection against electric shock under single fault conditions.”

Testing is not performed merely to fill a schedule.

Each result is evidence about a protective purpose.

Examples:

- CPC continuity → the protective path actually exists;
- insulation resistance → live conductors remain separated where they should;
- polarity → switching/protection is in the intended conductor;
- \(Z_s\) → the fault loop can support protective operation;
- RCD test → the residual-current protective device operates as required.

A strong candidate always connects:

\[
\boxed{\text{test}\rightarrow\text{measured quantity}\rightarrow\text{protective purpose}}
\]

# Section D — Testing Sequence and Dead vs Live Tests

## Question 21 — Correct Sequence
Four tests are listed:

1. insulation resistance;
2. continuity of conductors;
3. earth-fault loop impedance;
4. polarity.

Put them in the correct BS 7671 sequence. **(4 marks)**

### Answer

The correct order is:

\[
\boxed{2\rightarrow1\rightarrow4\rightarrow3}
\]

Therefore:

1. continuity of conductors;
2. insulation resistance;
3. polarity;
4. earth-fault loop impedance.

This follows the order of the relevant sections:

- 643.2 continuity;
- 643.3 insulation resistance;
- 643.6 polarity;
- 643.7 ADS/earth-fault loop verification.

---

## Question 22 — What If a Test Fails?
**What should be done if a test indicates a defect and remedial work is carried out? (5 marks)**

### Answer

The defect should first be corrected.

After the remedial work:

- the failed test must be repeated;
- any previous tests that could have been affected by the repair should also be repeated.

For example, if a conductor termination is repaired after continuity testing, it may be necessary to repeat:

- continuity;
- polarity;
- insulation resistance;
- and other affected verification.

The aim is to verify the **final condition of the installation**, not the condition before the repair.

---

## Question 23 — Dead Tests
**Give four examples of tests normally carried out with the circuit dead. (4 marks)**

### Answer

Examples include:

1. continuity of protective conductors;
2. ring-final-circuit continuity;
3. insulation resistance;
4. dead polarity testing;
5. continuity of bonding conductors.

Dead testing reduces exposure to energized conductors and should be used wherever it can provide the required verification.

---

## Question 24 — Live Tests
**Give four examples of tests or checks that may require the installation to be energized. (4 marks)**

### Answer

Examples include:

1. external earth-fault loop impedance \(Z_e\) by measurement;
2. supply polarity at the origin;
3. prospective fault current where measured;
4. RCD trip-time testing;
5. phase rotation where specifically required.

Live testing introduces additional danger and should be minimized.

---

## Question 25 — Why Minimize Live Testing?
**Explain why unnecessary live testing should be avoided during initial verification. (5 marks)**

### Answer

Live testing exposes the tester to:

- electric shock;
- short circuit;
- arc flash or burns;
- accidental contact with live parts.

Many quantities can be established by safer dead-test methods.

For example, circuit earth-fault loop impedance can often be determined from:

\[
Z_s=Z_e+(R_1+R_2)
\]

rather than carrying out a live loop test at every final point.

A competent tester should obtain the required evidence using the safest reasonable method.

---

# Section E — Continuity of Protective Conductors and Radial Circuits

## Question 26 — Purpose of Continuity Testing
**What does continuity testing of protective conductors verify? (4 marks)**

### Answer

It verifies that the protective conductor:

- exists;
- is electrically continuous;
- reaches the equipment that depends on it;
- provides a low-resistance protective path.

A visible green-and-yellow conductor is not proof of electrical continuity.

The conductor may be:

- broken;
- loose;
- incorrectly connected;
- disconnected at an intermediate point.

---

## Question 27 — Low-Resistance Test Instrument
**Why is a low-resistance continuity tester used for protective-conductor testing rather than an insulation-resistance tester? (4 marks)**

### Answer

Continuity testing is intended to measure very small conductor resistance.

A low-resistance ohmmeter is therefore designed to measure values such as:

\[
0.1\Omega,\quad0.5\Omega,\quad1.0\Omega
\]

with useful accuracy.

An insulation-resistance tester is intended to measure very large resistance values in:

\[
M\Omega
\]

and applies a much higher DC test voltage.

The two instruments/tests have completely different purposes.

---

## Question 28 — Radial \(R_1+R_2\) Test
**Describe how to measure \(R_1+R_2\) on a radial circuit. (8 marks)**

### Answer

A suitable method is:

1. safely isolate the circuit;
2. prove dead;
3. identify the circuit line conductor and CPC;
4. disconnect where necessary to avoid misleading parallel paths;
5. temporarily connect line and CPC together at the origin;
6. use a low-resistance continuity tester;
7. measure between line and CPC at each relevant point;
8. record the highest relevant value.

The measured test path includes:

\[
R_1+R_2
\]

where:

- \(R_1\) = line-conductor resistance;
- \(R_2\) = CPC resistance.

At the furthest point of a simple radial circuit the value is normally highest because the conductor path is longest.

---

## Question 29 — Predicting a Radial Result
A radial circuit has a combined line-and-CPC resistance of:

\[
28.5m\Omega/m
\]

The furthest point is 18 m from the origin.

Calculate the expected \(R_1+R_2\). **(5 marks)**

### Answer

\[
R_1+R_2=28.5m\Omega/m\times18m
\]

\[
=513m\Omega
\]

Convert to ohms:

\[
513m\Omega=0.513\Omega
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{R_1+R_2\approx0.51\Omega}
\]

If the measured result were dramatically different, the tester should investigate rather than simply record it.

# Section F — Lighting-Circuit Testing

## Question 30 — One-Way Lighting Continuity
**Explain how continuity and polarity can be checked on a simple one-way lighting circuit using a line-to-CPC link. (7 marks)**

### Answer

After safe isolation:

1. link the line conductor to the CPC at the origin;
2. operate the lighting switch to the ON position;
3. measure between the switched line at the lighting point and the CPC;
4. a low-resistance reading confirms the intended line path through the switch and the CPC return;
5. operate the switch OFF;
6. the line path should open;
7. this also helps confirm that the switch is interrupting the line conductor.

The measured low-resistance value to the relevant point may be recorded as the circuit:

\[
R_1+R_2
\]

where the test arrangement corresponds to that path.

---

## Question 31 — Why Test the Furthest Switch?
**Why is continuity of the CPC checked to the furthest relevant switch position or point on a lighting circuit? (4 marks)**

### Answer

The furthest point generally has:

- the longest conductor path;
- the greatest expected resistance;
- and therefore the most demanding continuity condition.

Testing only a nearby point could leave a break beyond that point undetected.

The protective conductor must be continuous throughout the circuit, including to metal accessories requiring earthing.

---

## Question 32 — Two-Way Lighting
**Why must the switch positions be operated during testing of a two-way lighting circuit? (5 marks)**

### Answer

Two-way switching provides alternative current paths through the strapper conductors.

If only one switch position is tested:

- one path may be verified;
- another path may remain untested;
- a broken strapper or incorrect connection could be missed.

Operating the switches systematically allows the tester to verify that:

- both switching paths operate correctly;
- the switched-line path is correct;
- the CPC remains continuous to the required points.

---

# Section G — Ring Final Circuit Continuity

## Question 33 — Three Main Ring Tests
**State the three main continuity stages used when testing a ring final circuit. (6 marks)**

### Answer

The three main stages are:

### Stage 1 — End-to-end continuity

Measure the end-to-end resistance of:

- line conductor:

\[
r_1
\]

- neutral conductor:

\[
r_n
\]

- CPC:

\[
r_2
\]

### Stage 2 — Line/neutral cross-connection

Cross-connect opposite ends of line and neutral and test at each socket.

### Stage 3 — Line/CPC cross-connection

Cross-connect opposite ends of line and CPC and test at each socket.

The third stage provides the ring-circuit:

\[
R_1+R_2
\]

value used in the test schedule.

---

## Question 34 — Expected \(r_1\) and \(r_n\)
A ring circuit uses the same conductor size and route for line and neutral.

The measured:

\[
r_1=0.82\Omega
\]

What approximate \(r_n\) would you expect, and why? **(4 marks)**

### Answer

Because line and neutral:

- have the same conductor material;
- have the same CSA;
- travel approximately the same route and length;

their resistance should be approximately equal.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{r_n\approx0.82\Omega}
\]

A large difference would require investigation.

---

## Question 35 — Expected \(r_2\)
A ring uses:

- 2.5 mm² line conductor;
- 1.5 mm² CPC.

If:

\[
r_1=0.90\Omega
\]

estimate \(r_2\) using the CSA ratio method. **(5 marks)**

### Answer

Approximate resistance ratio:

\[
\frac{2.5}{1.5}=1.67
\]

Therefore:

\[
r_2\approx1.67\times0.90
\]

\[
r_2\approx1.50\Omega
\]

Hence:

\[
\boxed{r_2\approx1.5\Omega}
\]

The smaller CPC has higher resistance because resistance increases as conductor cross-sectional area decreases.

---

## Question 36 — Line/Neutral Cross-Connection
A ring has:

\[
r_1=0.90\Omega
\]

and:

\[
r_n=0.90\Omega
\]

Calculate the expected line-to-neutral cross-connected reading. **(5 marks)**

### Answer

For a balanced ring:

\[
R_{L-N}\approx\frac{r_1+r_n}{4}
\]

Therefore:

\[
R_{L-N}=\frac{0.90+0.90}{4}
\]

\[
=\frac{1.80}{4}
\]

\[
\boxed{0.45\Omega}
\]

The readings around the ring should be broadly similar, allowing for small variations.

---

## Question 37 — Ring \(R_1+R_2\)
A ring has:

\[
r_1=0.90\Omega
\]

and:

\[
r_2=1.50\Omega
\]

Calculate the expected ring \(R_1+R_2\). **(5 marks)**

### Answer

Use:

\[
R_1+R_2\approx\frac{r_1+r_2}{4}
\]

Therefore:

\[
R_1+R_2=\frac{0.90+1.50}{4}
\]

\[
=\frac{2.40}{4}
\]

\[
\boxed{R_1+R_2=0.60\Omega}
\]

The highest appropriate measured value is normally recorded.

---

## Question 38 — Spur Indication
Most sockets on a ring produce:

\[
0.60\Omega
\]

during the line-CPC crossover test.

One socket gives:

\[
1.35\Omega
\]

What might this indicate? **(4 marks)**

### Answer

The significantly higher isolated reading may indicate:

- a spur from the ring;
- a poor termination;
- a faulty socket;
- unexpected conductor length;
- another wiring irregularity.

A spur is a strong possibility because its test path does not have the same balanced parallel-ring arrangement as sockets directly on the ring.

The result should be investigated rather than assumed to be acceptable.

---

## Question 39 — Wrong Cross-Connections
During the crossover test, readings gradually rise towards the middle of the ring and then fall again.

**What likely mistake should be investigated first? (4 marks)**

### Answer

The first possibility is incorrect cross-connection of the conductors at the origin.

For example, instead of connecting opposite ends:

- line 1 to CPC 2;
- line 2 to CPC 1;

the tester may have linked each line conductor to the CPC from the **same cable end**.

This produces a predictable rising-and-falling resistance pattern rather than the approximately balanced values expected from the correct cross-connection.

## Question 40 — Socket Switch OFF
During ring continuity testing, one switched socket gives an open-circuit reading.

What simple check should be made before assuming a conductor is broken? **(3 marks)**

### Answer

Check whether the socket switch is in the:

\[
\boxed{\text{ON position}}
\]

If the switch is OFF, the test path through the socket contacts may be open and the instrument may indicate very high or infinite resistance.

A competent tester always checks simple operating conditions before assuming a wiring defect.

# Section H — Insulation Resistance

## Question 41 — Purpose of an Insulation-Resistance Test
**What does insulation-resistance testing verify? (5 marks)**

### Answer

Insulation-resistance testing checks that electrical insulation adequately separates:

- live conductors from each other;
- live conductors from Earth/protective conductors.

It helps identify:

- damaged insulation;
- moisture;
- contamination;
- trapped conductors;
- incorrect connections;
- insulation breakdown;
- unwanted leakage paths.

A healthy installation should have **very high resistance** between conductors that should be electrically isolated.

---

## Question 42 — Table 64
**State the BS 7671 Table 64 test voltage and minimum insulation-resistance value for each of the following:**

**(a)** SELV/PELV  
**(b)** circuits up to and including 500 V, excluding SELV/PELV  
**(c)** circuits above 500 V  

**(9 marks)**

### Answer

From **BS 7671 Table 64**:

| Circuit nominal voltage | Test voltage DC | Minimum insulation resistance |
|---|---:|---:|
| SELV and PELV | \(\boxed{250V}\) | \(\boxed{0.5M\Omega}\) |
| Up to and including 500 V, excluding SELV/PELV | \(\boxed{500V}\) | \(\boxed{1.0M\Omega}\) |
| Above 500 V | \(\boxed{1000V}\) | \(\boxed{1.0M\Omega}\) |

These are **minimum acceptance values**, not typical values expected on a healthy new circuit.

---

## Question 43 — Standard 230/400 V Installation
A normal 230/400 V low-voltage installation is being insulation-resistance tested.

What test voltage and minimum value normally apply? **(4 marks)**

### Answer

The installation falls into:

\[
\boxed{\text{up to and including 500 V}}
\]

Therefore Table 64 gives:

\[
\boxed{500V\text{ DC test voltage}}
\]

and minimum insulation resistance:

\[
\boxed{1M\Omega}
\]

A healthy new installation would normally be expected to produce a value much higher than this minimum.

---

## Question 44 — Why “Higher Is Better”
**Why is a high insulation-resistance reading desirable? (4 marks)**

### Answer

High resistance means very little current can leak through the insulation.

Ideally, insulation behaves as an extremely high-resistance barrier between conductors.

Therefore:

\[
R_{\text{insulation}}\uparrow
\]

means:

\[
I_{\text{leakage}}\downarrow
\]

A low insulation-resistance value suggests that an unwanted conductive path may exist.

---

## Question 45 — Electronic Equipment and SPDs
**What should be considered before applying a 500 V DC insulation-resistance test to a circuit containing electronic equipment or an SPD? (6 marks)**

### Answer

The test voltage may:

- damage sensitive electronic components;
- cause SPDs to conduct;
- produce misleadingly low test results;
- stress equipment not intended to receive the test voltage.

Therefore equipment likely to influence the result or be damaged should normally be:

\[
\boxed{\text{disconnected before testing}}
\]

Where appropriate, insulation resistance should be tested during erection before sensitive equipment is connected.

---

## Question 46 — Reduced Test Voltage
Where it is not reasonably practicable to disconnect equipment that may be affected by a 500 V test, what alternative does BS 7671 permit for the particular circuit? **(5 marks)**

### Answer

The test voltage may be reduced to:

\[
\boxed{250V\text{ DC}}
\]

but the insulation resistance must still be at least:

\[
\boxed{1M\Omega}
\]

The 250 V test is not an excuse to accept poor insulation.

It is intended to protect connected equipment that might otherwise be damaged by the higher test voltage.

---

## Question 47 — Single-Phase Test Combinations
A single-phase circuit has line, neutral and CPC.

**State the insulation-resistance combinations that may need to be verified. (4 marks)**

### Answer

The relevant conductor relationships include:

1. line to neutral;
2. line to CPC/Earth;
3. neutral to CPC/Earth.

Where sensitive connected equipment is present, line and neutral may be connected together and tested collectively to Earth where the applicable method permits.

The purpose is to demonstrate adequate insulation between conductors that should not be electrically connected.

---

## Question 48 — Three-Phase Insulation-Resistance Sequence
A three-phase four-wire circuit has L1, L2, L3, N and CPC.

**List a systematic sequence that checks every unique insulation-resistance conductor pair. (12 marks)**

### Answer

There are ten unique tests.

### To CPC/Earth

1. L1-E
2. L2-E
3. L3-E
4. N-E

### To Neutral

5. L1-N
6. L2-N
7. L3-N

### Between Lines

8. L1-L2
9. L1-L3
10. L2-L3

A systematic method is preferable to random testing because every completed group allows one conductor to be removed from further combinations.

For example:

- finish all tests to Earth;
- then all remaining tests to neutral;
- then L1 to remaining lines;
- finally L2-L3.

## Question 49 — Low Insulation Reading
A new circuit gives:

\[
0.42M\Omega
\]

at 500 V DC.

The applicable Table 64 minimum is:

\[
1M\Omega
\]

What should be done? **(5 marks)**

### Answer

The result is unsatisfactory because:

\[
0.42M\Omega<1M\Omega
\]

The circuit should **not** simply be energized and accepted.

The cause should be investigated.

Possible causes include:

- damaged insulation;
- connected loads;
- SPDs;
- electronic equipment;
- moisture;
- trapped cable;
- conductor contact;
- incorrect termination.

The defect must be corrected and the affected tests repeated.

## Question 50 — Locating an Insulation Fault
An entire installation gives a low insulation-resistance reading.

**Explain a practical method of locating the faulty section. (6 marks)**

### Answer

A systematic approach is:

1. isolate and safely prove dead;
2. divide the installation into smaller sections or circuits;
3. disconnect circuits as necessary;
4. test each section separately;
5. compare the results;
6. continue subdividing the faulty section until the defective circuit or equipment is identified.

This method converts one large unknown fault into a series of smaller identifiable sections.

# Section I — Polarity

## Question 51 — Purpose of Polarity Verification
**State four things that polarity verification is intended to confirm. (4 marks)**

### Answer

Polarity verification helps confirm that:

1. line conductors are connected to the correct line terminals;
2. neutral conductors are connected to neutral terminals;
3. single-pole switches interrupt the line conductor;
4. fuses and single-pole protective devices are in the line conductor;
5. socket-outlet terminals are correctly connected;
6. lighting accessories are wired so exposed or normally neutral parts are not left live unnecessarily.

---

## Question 52 — Why Switch the Line?
A lighting switch has been installed in the neutral conductor instead of the line conductor.

**Explain why this is dangerous even if the lamp turns OFF normally. (5 marks)**

### Answer

Opening the neutral stops current, so the lamp goes out.

However, the line conductor may remain connected through the lamp circuit.

Parts of the lamp holder or fitting may therefore remain at line potential even though the lamp appears OFF.

A person replacing a lamp or working on the fitting could receive an electric shock.

Therefore a single-pole switch should interrupt the:

\[
\boxed{\text{line conductor}}
\]

---

## Question 53 — Reverse Polarity at a Socket
**What is meant by reverse polarity at a socket-outlet, and why is it unsafe? (5 marks)**

### Answer

Reverse polarity commonly means that line and neutral conductors are connected to the wrong terminals.

The appliance may still operate because current can still flow.

However:

- switches may interrupt neutral instead of line;
- internal parts expected to be neutral may remain live;
- fuses may be placed in the wrong conductor;
- safe isolation assumptions can become dangerous.

Correct operation of equipment is therefore not proof of correct polarity.

---

## Question 54 — Dead Polarity Testing
**How can an \(R_1+R_2\) continuity arrangement help verify circuit polarity before energization? (5 marks)**

### Answer

If line and CPC are linked at the origin, a low-resistance test at the correct line terminal of each accessory can verify that the conductor identified as line actually reaches that line terminal.

Examples include checking:

- socket line terminals;
- switch common/line terminals;
- switched-line points.

This can verify much of the circuit polarity while the installation remains dead.

The supply polarity at the origin must still be verified by the appropriate method.

---

# Section J — Earth-Fault Loop and Prospective Fault Current During Verification

## Question 55 — Safer Determination of \(Z_s\)
**Explain how circuit \(Z_s\) can be determined without carrying out a live loop-impedance test at every point. (5 marks)**

### Answer

Measure or otherwise determine the external loop impedance:

\[
Z_e
\]

Then obtain the circuit:

\[
R_1+R_2
\]

by dead continuity testing.

Calculate:

\[
\boxed{Z_s=Z_e+(R_1+R_2)}
\]

This method:

- reduces live testing;
- uses the actual circuit continuity result;
- can be repeated for every circuit;
- avoids unnecessary exposure to live terminals.

---

## Question 56 — Why Not Calculate \(R_1+R_2\) by Subtracting Live Measurements?
**Why is it generally unsound to calculate \(R_1+R_2\) simply by subtracting measured \(Z_e\) from measured \(Z_s\)? (5 marks)**

### Answer

Measured \(Z_s\) may include **parallel earth paths** such as:

- bonding;
- structural metalwork;
- metallic services;
- other protective conductors.

These parallel paths can lower the measured \(Z_s\).

The measured \(Z_e\) may have been obtained with bonding paths excluded.

Therefore:

\[
Z_s-Z_e
\]

may not represent the true resistance of the circuit line conductor plus CPC.

\(R_1+R_2\) should be established by an appropriate dead continuity test.

---

## Question 57 — Prospective Fault Current
**What is prospective fault current (PFC)? (5 marks)**

### Answer

Prospective fault current is the current that would flow at a point in an installation under a fault of negligible impedance.

Relevant fault currents include:

- prospective short-circuit current between live conductors;
- prospective earth-fault current from line to Earth.

The value is important because protective devices and switchgear must have adequate:

- breaking capacity;
- short-circuit withstand;
- fault-current rating.

Where more than one type of prospective fault current can occur, the relevant highest value is used for equipment selection.

---

# Section K — RCD Testing

## Question 58 — Current BS 7671 RCD Test
For a general non-delay RCD under BS 7671:2018+A2:2022+A3:2024, what test is used to verify operating time? **(5 marks)**

### Answer

An alternating-current test is applied at the RCD's rated residual operating current:

\[
\boxed{I_{\Delta n}}
\]

For a general non-delay RCD, effective operation is verified where the RCD disconnects within:

\[
\boxed{300ms}
\]

This applies regardless of whether the RCD is Type AC, A, F or B for the purpose of this BS 7671 verification test.

---

## Question 59 — Is 5× \(I_{\Delta n}\) Still Required?
**Does BS 7671:2018+A2:2022 require the traditional test at five times rated residual current for verification? Explain. (4 marks)**

### Answer

No.

The previous routine requirement to perform a:

\[
5\times I_{\Delta n}
\]

test was removed by Amendment 2.

For current BS 7671 verification under the Brown Book framework, the key operating-time verification is the AC test at:

\[
\boxed{I_{\Delta n}}
\]

with the appropriate maximum time.

Older training material may still show the former 5× test sequence, so the edition of the regulations must always be checked.

---

## Question 60 — S-Type RCD
**What operating-time range applies to an S-type time-delayed RCD under the relevant BS 7671 verification guidance? (3 marks)**

### Answer

For an S-type time-delayed RCD:

\[
\boxed{130ms\leq t\leq500ms}
\]

An S-type RCD is not used for additional protection where the requirements demand the faster additional-protection function.

---

## Question 61 — Test Button vs Instrument Test
**Explain the difference between pressing an RCD test button and carrying out an instrument trip-time test. (5 marks)**

### Answer

### Test button

The integral test button:

- checks the internal tripping mechanism;
- creates an internal imbalance;
- confirms that the device can mechanically/electrically trip.

### Instrument test

An RCD tester applies a controlled residual test current through the installation and measures the actual operating time.

Therefore the instrument test verifies quantitative performance such as:

\[
\text{trip time in milliseconds}
\]

The test button is important, but it does not replace all required verification by test instrument.

---

## Question 62 — Where to Test an RCD
**Why may testing at or close to the outgoing terminals of the RCD give a more controlled verification result? (4 marks)**

### Answer

Testing close to the RCD can reduce the influence of:

- downstream leakage currents;
- connected equipment;
- circuit capacitance;
- other installation effects.

The test then assesses the device more directly.

However, safe access and the test-instrument manufacturer's instructions must always be considered.

For some circuits, a plug-in test method at a suitable socket may reduce exposure to live terminals.

# Section L — Phase Sequence and Functional Testing

## Question 63 — Phase Sequence
**What is phase sequence in a three-phase installation? (4 marks)**

### Answer

Phase sequence is the order in which the three phase voltages reach their corresponding points in the AC cycle.

A typical sequence may be:

\[
\boxed{L1\rightarrow L2\rightarrow L3}
\]

Maintaining phase sequence is important where equipment depends on phase order.

Incorrect sequence can affect:

- motor direction;
- rotating equipment;
- phase-sensitive control systems.

---

## Question 64 — Phase Sequence vs Phase Rotation
**Distinguish between phase sequence and checking the direction of motor rotation. (5 marks)**

### Answer

Phase sequence concerns the maintained order of the phase conductors throughout a polyphase installation.

It can often be verified by:

- conductor identification;
- correct termination;
- systematic inspection.

Motor rotation is the **mechanical direction** in which a connected motor turns when energized.

A phase-rotation test may be required for practical commissioning of equipment, but it is not identical to verifying conductor phase sequence.

---

## Question 65 — Functional Testing
**State six items that may require functional testing after electrical safety tests have been completed. (6 marks)**

### Answer

Examples include:

1. switches;
2. isolators;
3. controlgear;
4. interlocks;
5. emergency switching;
6. RCD test facilities;
7. AFDD test facilities where present;
8. contactors;
9. motor controls;
10. changeover mechanisms;
11. protective-device operating controls.

Functional testing verifies that equipment actually performs its intended operational function.

# Section M — Test-Result Interpretation and Fault Finding

# Step 4 Distinction Lens — Never Record a Number Blindly

Before pressing TEST, predict the approximate pattern you expect.

Ask:

- should this be low resistance or high resistance?
- should the reading rise with circuit length?
- should line and neutral end-to-end values be similar?
- should a smaller CPC have higher resistance?
- should insulation resistance be extremely high?
- is a live \(Z_s\) lower than the dead-calculated value because of parallel paths?

The strongest tester is not the person who can operate the meter fastest. It is the person who can explain whether the displayed number makes electrical sense.

---

## Question 66 — Expected Result vs Measured Result
**Why should a competent tester predict an approximate result before measuring? (5 marks)**

### Answer

A predicted result provides a reasonableness check.

If the expected value is approximately:

\[
0.50\Omega
\]

but the instrument displays:

\[
5.00\Omega
\]

the tester immediately knows that something requires investigation.

Possible causes include:

- incorrect test connections;
- wrong circuit;
- loose terminal;
- broken conductor;
- incorrect conductor size;
- faulty accessory;
- instrument problem.

Without an expected value, the tester may record a meaningless number simply because the meter displayed it.

---

## Question 67 — Very Low Live \(Z_s\)
A calculated circuit value from:

\[
Z_e+(R_1+R_2)
\]

is 0.92 Ω.

A live loop test gives 0.48 Ω.

Give a likely explanation. **(4 marks)**

### Answer

The lower live reading may be caused by:

\[
\boxed{\text{parallel earth-return paths}}
\]

Examples include:

- bonding conductors;
- metallic services;
- structural steel;
- cable armour;
- interconnected CPCs.

Parallel paths reduce the measured loop impedance.

The difference does not automatically prove the dead-test calculation is wrong.

---

## Question 68 — Broken Neutral: Voltage Clue
On a radial socket circuit, a socket does not operate a lamp.

With the lamp plugged in:

- line to Earth = approximately 230 V;
- line to neutral = approximately 0 V;
- neutral to Earth = approximately 230 V.

What fault should be suspected, and explain the readings. **(8 marks)**

### Answer

A likely fault is:

\[
\boxed{\text{broken/open neutral upstream of the socket}}
\]

The line conductor remains connected to the supply.

Because the neutral is broken, it no longer has a direct zero-volt reference to the supply neutral.

With the load connected, the line voltage is transferred through the lamp/load to the disconnected neutral conductor.

Therefore the local neutral can rise towards line potential.

At the faulty socket:

\[
L\approx230V
\]

and:

\[
N\approx230V
\]

relative to Earth.

The difference between them is then:

\[
L-N\approx0V
\]

so the appliance cannot receive the required potential difference and does not operate normally.

This is also a reminder that a neutral conductor must never be assumed safe merely because it is called “neutral.”

---

## Question 69 — Borrowed Neutral
**Explain why a borrowed or shared neutral between two separately protected circuits can create a serious isolation hazard. (6 marks)**

### Answer

If one circuit is isolated but its neutral is shared with another circuit that remains energized:

- current from the energized circuit may continue to flow through the shared neutral;
- the supposedly isolated circuit may still contain dangerous potential;
- an RCD may operate unexpectedly;
- conductors may become energized from another source.

A person could isolate one MCB correctly and still be exposed to danger.

This is why:

- circuits should be correctly identified;
- shared neutrals must be recognized;
- all relevant sources must be isolated;
- complete proving-dead checks are essential.

## Question 74 — Practical Assessment Scenario
An electrician begins a test assessment by switching an MCB OFF and immediately disconnecting the circuit conductors.

Identify the main safety failure and state the correct actions that should have preceded disconnection. **(8 marks)**

### Answer

The main failure is:

\[
\boxed{\text{failure to complete safe isolation and prove dead}}
\]

The electrician should have:

2. inspected the voltage indicator/proving equipment;

5. locked off the isolating device;

7. tested all relevant conductor combinations for absence of voltage;
8. re-proved the voltage indicator.

Only then should conductors be treated as dead.

## Question 76 — Oral Interview: Explain Testing from Start to Finish
An examiner asks:

> **“You have completed a new domestic circuit. Explain how you would inspect, test and commission it from the moment you arrive at the board until handover.”**

Give a complete answer. **(20 marks)**

### Answer

A strong answer should be structured.

### 1. Confirm the Work and Circuit

Identify:

- circuit purpose;
- design;
- cable type;
- protective device;
- earthing arrangement;
- any special equipment.

---

### 2. Safe Isolation

Before dismantling or dead testing:

- identify the circuit;
- prove the voltage indicator;
- isolate;
- lock off;
- prove dead using all relevant conductor combinations;
- re-prove the voltage indicator.

---

### 3. Visual Inspection

Check:

- equipment condition;
- conductor sizes;
- protective devices;
- terminations;
- conductor identification;
- CPC connections;
- enclosure integrity;
- cable support;
- mechanical protection;
- environmental suitability.

---

### 4. Dead Testing

Carry out the relevant tests in a logical sequence.

These may include:

- continuity of protective conductors;
- ring-final continuity where applicable;
- \(R_1+R_2\);
- insulation resistance;
- dead polarity;
- other separation tests where relevant.

Record results as testing proceeds.

---

### 5. Correct Any Defects

If a result is unsatisfactory:

- stop;
- investigate;
- correct the defect;
- repeat the failed test;
- repeat any earlier test affected by the repair.

Do not continue simply to complete the form.

---

### 6. Prepare for Energization

Before energization:

- restore all temporary links;
- reconnect conductors correctly;
- confirm covers and barriers are in place;
- ensure nobody is exposed to danger;
- remove test arrangements not intended for service.

---

### 7. Live Verification

Carry out only the live tests that are necessary.

Depending on the installation these may include:

- supply polarity at origin;
- \(Z_e\);
- PFC;
- RCD operating test;
- phase-related checks where relevant.

Where possible calculate:

\[
Z_s=Z_e+(R_1+R_2)
\]

rather than unnecessarily live-loop testing every point.

---

### 8. Functional Testing

Operate:

- switches;
- isolators;
- RCD test buttons;
- controlgear;
- interlocks;
- changeover devices;
- other relevant controls.

Verify correct function.

---

### 9. Final Assessment

Review the test results as a complete set.

Ask:

- do the values make sense?
- is ADS effective?
- is polarity correct?
- is insulation satisfactory?
- are protective conductors continuous?
- are protective devices suitable?
- is the installation safe for service?

---

### 10. Documentation and Handover

Complete the required:

- test records;
- commissioning information;
- circuit schedules;
- labels;
- certification/reporting documentation.

Explain relevant operation and safety information to the client where required.

The core principle is:

\[
\boxed{\text{Inspect}\rightarrow\text{Test}\rightarrow\text{Interpret}\rightarrow\text{Correct}\rightarrow\text{Commission}\rightarrow\text{Document}}
\]

# BS 7671 Reference Summary for Step 4

## Regulation 641.1 — Initial Verification

An installation is inspected and tested during erection and on completion before being put into service.

---

## Regulation 643.1 — Testing

Relevant tests in Regulations 643.2 to 643.11 are carried out and results compared with the appropriate criteria.

A logical sequence is important because later tests may depend on satisfactory completion of earlier tests.

---

## Regulation 643.2 — Continuity

Continuity of protective conductors and relevant ring-final conductors is verified by measurement.

---

## Regulation 643.3 / Table 64 — Insulation Resistance

| Circuit nominal voltage | Test voltage | Minimum IR |
|---|---:|---:|
| SELV / PELV | 250 V DC | 0.5 MΩ |
| Up to and including 500 V, excluding SELV/PELV | 500 V DC | 1.0 MΩ |
| Above 500 V | 1000 V DC | 1.0 MΩ |

Where equipment likely to be affected cannot reasonably be disconnected, a reduced 250 V DC test may be used for the particular circuit, but the minimum remains:

\[
\boxed{1M\Omega}
\]

---

## Regulation 643.6 — Polarity

Polarity is verified so that conductors and single-pole protective/control devices are connected in the intended conductors.

Supply polarity at the origin is verified before the installation is energized as required by the regulation.

---

## Regulations 643.7 and 643.8 — ADS and Additional Protection

Verification may include:

- earth-electrode resistance;
- earth-fault loop impedance;
- RCD operation;
- protective-conductor continuity;
- prospective fault current.

Where appropriate, \(Z_s\) can be determined by:

\[
\boxed{Z_s=Z_e+(R_1+R_2)}
\]

to reduce unnecessary live testing.

---

## Current RCD Verification under BS 7671:2018+A2:2022+A3:2024

For a general non-delay RCD, an AC test at:

\[
\boxed{I_{\Delta n}}
\]

is used.

Maximum operating time:

\[
\boxed{300ms}
\]

The previous routine requirement for a:

\[
5\times I_{\Delta n}
\]

test was removed by Amendment 2.

For S-type time-delayed RCDs:

\[
\boxed{130ms\leq t\leq500ms}
\]

---

## Regulation 643.9 — Phase Sequence

For polyphase circuits, phase sequence is verified at relevant points.

This should not be confused with the separate practical task of checking the mechanical rotation direction of a motor.

---

## Regulation 643.10 — Functional Testing

Relevant assemblies and equipment such as:

- switchgear;
- controlgear;
- interlocks;
- drives;
- test facilities

are functionally tested where required.

---

## Regulation 643.11 — Voltage Drop

Voltage drop is verified where required.

For many installations it can be established from design calculations and circuit information rather than by a separate direct voltage-drop measurement.

---

# Safe-Isolation Reference Summary

For proving dead on a low-voltage circuit, good practice is to use a suitable:

\[
\boxed{\text{two-pole voltage detector}}
\]

and apply:

\[
\boxed{\text{prove tester}\rightarrow\text{prove dead}\rightarrow\text{re-prove tester}}
\]

For single phase, check:

\[
L-N,\quad L-E,\quad N-E
\]

For L1, L2, L3, N and E, a complete systematic check contains ten unique conductor combinations.

You should now be able to:

1. give the formal definitions of verification, inspection and isolation;
2. explain safe isolation as more than switching OFF;
3. prove a voltage indicator before and after proving dead;
4. explain why L-N alone is not enough;
5. state the logical dead-before-live test sequence and explain why;
6. connect every test to the protection it verifies;
7. predict ring/radial test-result patterns before measuring;

9. explain current RCD verification without repeating obsolete test routines;
