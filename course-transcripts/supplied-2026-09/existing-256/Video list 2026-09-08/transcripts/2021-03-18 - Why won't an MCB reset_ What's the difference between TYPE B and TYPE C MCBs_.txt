the right time they've cleared a fault and then they just won't reset afterwards but why won't they reset well there's a few reasons that this may be happening the first most obvious reason is that the fault actually hasn't been cleared it's still in the system so if the fault either in the cabling or the equipment is still there then the mcb obviously won't reset now that's a bit of an obvious statement for an electrician and you're more than likely to have already cleared the fault before you attempt to reset the circuit breaker so is there another more subtle reason that the mcb won't reset well it may be that your mcb has experienced a fault that means it will never reset looking at table 7.2.71 of the onsite guide published by the iet we find that mcbs according to bs en 60898 have two values for their short circuit rating capacity the first is the icn or rated short circuit capacity and the second is the ics or service short circuit capacity what's the difference the rated short circuit capacity or icn is the maximum amount of current the circuit breaker can trip safely when a short circuit or fault of negligible impedance occurs this is generally a large amount of current and most mcbs will have a value of 6 000 amps or 10 000 amps now you can imagine that this is a lot of current to flow and if the value of fault current is more than this then the mcb may not rating the ics well remember this is the service short circuit capacity rather than the rated short circuit capacity and this indicates the amount of current that the mcb can disconnect and be relied upon to operate again after the fault has been cleared and the mcb reset looking at that table again we can see that for an mcb with a 10 kilo amp icn value the service short circuit capacity is only 7.5 kilo amps this means that if the mcb is protecting a circuit and a fault current with a value between 7.5 ka and 10 ka flows let's say 9 ka this current is below the rated short circuit capacity of the breaker which means it will disconnect safely however it's above the value of the service short circuit capacity of the circuit breaker which means it may not trip again in the future and indeed you may not even be able to reset the mcb it may just keep tripping off even when the circuit is healthy and actually the mcb will need replacing there is another reason that the mcb may keep tripping and this time it's not because the circuit has a fault or the circuit breaker has gone over its service short circuit capacity value or that the mcb is faulty it may simply be that the mcb and the load are mismatched what do we mean by this well mcbs that are manufactured according to bscn60898 come in three different types denoted by the letters b c and d there's no type a as it could get mixed up with the a for amps that comes after the rating on the breaker now traditionally some electricians will say that type b's are for domestic installations that you use type c's in commercial properties and type d's are to be used in industrial applications now there is a shred of truth in this but it's not actually the way that the different types are supposed to be used the type of mcb you use is determined by the type of load that you connect it to certain loads experience a phenomenon called inrush current now a load that has no inrush current is protected by a type b circuit breaker one that experiences a moderate amount of inrush current is protected by a type c and you'd use a type d circuit breaker to supply a load that experiences high values of inrush current so if you've got for example say a b type mcb protecting a load that should be protected by a c type then the inrush current could cause the mcb to trip every time the load is switched on so why does this myth about the domestic commercial and industrial applications of the different types of mcb persist and how do you know how much inrush current a load is likely to suffer from what's the
