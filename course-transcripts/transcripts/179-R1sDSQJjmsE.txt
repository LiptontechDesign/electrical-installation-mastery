In table 537.4, we find a list of different devices that are designed to disconnect electricity. We've got the device type, the standard that the device conforms to, and then three different types of disconnection. isolation, emergency, and functional. Looking at this pull cord, you can see that it conforms to BSEN 60669-1, which covers the general requirements for switches for household and similar fixed electrical installations. If we look up BSEN 60669-1 in table 537.4, we can see that it cannot in fact be used for isolation, nor can it be used for emergency switching. So, if something's gone horribly wrong and someone's getting a shock in the shower, it's not designed to disconnect the supply in an emergency. It might well perform the function, but it's not an emergency switch. There is an argument that suggests that if you, the electrician, has switched the shower off at the pull cord to do some maintenance on the shower, then the supply is under your control the whole time and can therefore be said to be isolated. But we'll debunk that in a moment. The reality is that the only thing that it can be used for is plain old functional switching, i.e. turning the shower on and off. And this is borne out for us on the packaging it came in. It clearly says it's a switch. But if it's only there for functional switching, and most electric showers tend to have an on off switch built into them for functional purposes, do we need to install one at all? Well, yes, we do. This is where we circle back to the much discussed, much under-appreciated, and much misunderstood manufacturers instructions. Regulation 134.1.1 reminds us of the importance of good workmanship. Although with it being 2025, we probably should be referring to good working practice by now. But it then goes on to stress the importance of taking account of manufacturers's instructions. So if we head over to a set of typical instructions from a shower manufacturer, this is for the Mira Azora. It tells us the following. A separate permanently connected supply must be taken from the consumer unit to the appliance through a double pole switch which has at least 3 mm of contact separation. The switch can be a ceiling mounted pull cord type within the shower room or a wall-mounted switch in an adjacent room. So, a double pole switch is required. The bit where it suggests a wall-mounted switch in an adjacent room would suggest that this switch is in fact not intended at all for isolation and maintenance on parts that could become live because it's definitely not under your control then. So, it can only be there for functional switching purposes. We need to install it due to some further direction in the instructions. The manufacturer actually gives the user a detailed sequence of steps for showering. You know, minus the lathering, rinsing, and brooding on the futility of it all. It tells us to switch on electrical supply at the pull cord or switch. Then press the start stop button on the shower itself. Next, there's various steps on setting temperatures and flow rates and so forth. Upon finishing, we press stop button with the caveat that the shower will continue to run for a few seconds before stopping. And then we terrible choice of words in this context isolate the shower or switch it off at the pull cord. So the shower isolator or more accurately switch is just there to disconnect the power to the shower when it's not in use. And it's not just this shower manufacturer who arbitrarily decided that this should be the case. When making an appliance like a shower, they're bound by product standards. One of which is 60335-1 household and similar electrical appliances. Safety part one general requirement states 7.12.2 If a stationary appliance is not fitted with a supply cord and a plug or with other means for disconnection from the supply mains having a contact separation in all poles that provide full disconnection under over-voltage category 3 conditions. The instructions shall state that means for disconnection must be incorporated in the fixed wiring in accordance with the wiring rules. So the manufacturers have to pop a statement into their instructions saying that some kind of local disconnection needs to be provided to allow it to be disconnected. The mention of a plug suggests it means local functional switching and we can't use an MCB or RCBO for that. So that's where that direction in the shower instructions about a switch comes from. So we should fit some kind of functional switch. We could indeed pop in a shower pull cord. CED Electrical Group provide really sturdy low profile ones for just this purpose.
